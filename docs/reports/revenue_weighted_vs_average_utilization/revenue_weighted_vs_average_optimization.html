This report answers a different question from the earlier revenue-weighted report:

For the same clinic background and the same policy regime, what changes when the optimizer targets average utilization instead of revenue-weighted service?

The comparison is therefore objective-to-objective, not simply policy-to-baseline.

The Revenue-Weighted Service Objective is

Rα=αY1+Y2, R_\alpha=\alpha Y_1+Y_2,

where Y1Y_1 and Y2Y_2 are completed Class 1 and Class 2 visits. We evaluate α=1.3,1.6,\alpha=1.3,1.6, and 2.02.0. The multiplier is a relative service-value assumption.

At α=1\alpha=1, maximizing RαR_\alpha is ranking-equivalent to maximizing total completed visits and therefore average utilization within a clinic background.

1 Comparison design

The expanded experiment contains 5,670 clinic backgrounds. Headline comparisons use the 3,600 heterogeneous main-range backgrounds with ρ≤2.5\rho\leq2.5. Same-behavior controls are excluded from all headline summaries.

For every background and policy regime:

  1. the average-utilization optimizer selects its best policy cell on the five search seeds;
  2. the revenue-weighted optimizer independently selects its best policy cell for a given α\alpha;
  3. both frozen cells are evaluated on the same ten independent evaluation seeds;
  4. this report compares their evaluated metrics within the same background.

The primary quantities below are therefore matched differences:

Revenue-optimized outcome−Average-utilization-optimized outcome. \text{Revenue-optimized outcome}- \text{Average-utilization-optimized outcome}.

This is the cleanest way to answer how changing the objective changes the policy and its consequences.

Policy regimes remain:

Both flexible Optimized Quantity and window optimized

Unused protected capacity is released on the service day.

The core result is straightforward:

Switching from average-utilization optimization to revenue-weighted optimization buys additional weighted service primarily by reallocating access toward Class 1, not by serving more patients overall.

Under both-flexible optimization:

  • extra RαR_\alpha rises from +0.77% at α=1.3\alpha=1.3 to +2.08% at α=2\alpha=2;
  • average utilization changes by only −0.01 to −0.03 pp;
  • Class 1 served rate rises another 4.4–4.7 pp;
  • Class 2 served rate falls another 6.7–7.6 pp.

These are medians of within-background differences, so they directly compare the two optimization objectives in the same scenarios.

2.1 Why horizon-only changes little

The median head-to-head difference for horizon-only is zero at all three weights. Revenue weighting can select a different horizon in some backgrounds, but in more than half the backgrounds the evaluated outcomes are the same or essentially equivalent.

The objective switch becomes consequential when the optimizer can use reservations, because reservations provide a direct mechanism to redirect scarce capacity toward Class 1.

Average-utilization optimization already creates some revenue-weighted gain because it increases completions and, in some backgrounds, changes the class mix. Revenue optimization goes substantially further by explicitly rewarding Class 1 completions.

The key contrast is that average utilization remains essentially unchanged between the two objectives, while class-specific access changes markedly.

4 Access is the main margin of adjustment

The near-zero utilization bars are the central feature of the comparison. The revenue optimizer is not finding a large new source of unused capacity. Instead, it is changing which class occupies the available capacity.

At α=1.6\alpha=1.6, for example, switching objectives within the same background has median effects of:

  • R1.6R_{1.6}: +1.41% relative to the utilization-optimized policy;
  • average utilization: −0.02 pp;
  • Class 1 served rate: +4.61 pp;
  • Class 2 served rate: −7.07 pp.

The weighted objective therefore behaves primarily as a prioritization objective, whereas average-utilization optimization behaves primarily as an efficiency objective.

5 Scenario-by-scenario access tradeoff

At α=1.6\alpha=1.6:

  • 96.9% of backgrounds have a higher Class 1 served rate under revenue optimization;
  • 96.7% have a lower Class 2 served rate;
  • 61.3% have slightly lower average utilization, but the typical utilization change is only −0.02 pp;
  • 95.0% have a higher evaluated R1.6R_{1.6}.

The scatter makes the mechanism visible: most scenarios move toward higher Class 1 access and lower Class 2 access when the objective changes.

Thus, the two objectives almost never choose the same both-flexible policy cell once Class 1 carries meaningful extra value.

The clearest policy change is the protected quantity:

Average-utilization optimization uses a median reservation quantity around 72% of daily capacity, whereas unconstrained revenue optimization moves to 100% of daily capacity.

Same-day release prevents unused protected capacity from being permanently stranded, but the larger reservation still substantially changes who receives appointments before release.

As α\alpha rises, the revenue optimizer also uses somewhat longer horizons and reservation windows. These settings give the optimizer more scope to preserve Class 1 access while maintaining a large protected quantity.

This gives the objective comparison a useful operational interpretation:

  • When Class 1 is only 10% of arrivals, revenue optimization can increase Class 1 access by about 4.3 pp while the median additional Class 2 loss stays around 0.5 pp.
  • At intermediate-to-high Class 1 shares, substantially more capacity must be redirected to generate additional weighted value.
  • The largest incremental revenue gain occurs around 70% Class 1 share, but it comes with a very large additional Class 2 access loss.

The economics of the objective therefore depend strongly on how large the priority population is.

8 How to interpret the objective choice

The comparison is not “revenue optimization is better” or “utilization optimization is better.” The two objectives answer different operational questions.

Average-utilization optimization asks:

Which policy produces the most completed visits from available capacity?

Its main lever is shorter booking horizons in congested, delay-sensitive clinics.

Revenue-weighted optimization asks:

Which policy produces the most weighted service when a Class 1 completion is worth more?

Once reservations are available, the optimizer can increase that objective without materially increasing total completions by replacing some Class 2 service with Class 1 service.

The clinic therefore faces a real objective tradeoff:

  • higher weighted service;
  • almost unchanged total utilization;
  • higher Class 1 access;
  • lower Class 2 access.

9 Clinic-facing conclusions

  • The relevant comparison is objective-to-objective within the same scenarios. Relative to the average-utilization-optimal both-flexible policy, revenue optimization adds a median 0.77%, 1.41%, and 2.08% in RαR_\alpha for α=1.3,1.6,2.0\alpha=1.3,1.6,2.0.
  • The additional weighted value does not come from higher utilization. Median utilization changes by only −0.01 to −0.03 pp when the objective switches.
  • The main change is access redistribution. Revenue optimization raises Class 1 served rate by another 4.4–4.7 pp and lowers Class 2 served rate by another 6.7–7.6 pp under both-flexible policies.
  • Reservation flexibility is what makes the objective switch consequential. Horizon-only differences are typically zero; reservation-only differences are modest; both-flexible differences are much larger.
  • Revenue optimization selects much more aggressive protection. Median QQ/capacity rises from about 72% to 100%.
  • The access cost depends strongly on Class 1 share. When Class 1 is rare, additional Class 2 harm can remain small; at larger Class 1 shares, the redistribution becomes substantial.
  • Average-utilization optimization is the cleaner objective when total throughput is the priority. Revenue weighting is appropriate only if the clinic intentionally values a Class 1 completion more and accepts the corresponding distributional consequences.
